1 / AI and learning

AI-Induced Never-Skilling in Medical Education

A Nature Medicine Perspective introduces never-skilling: the risk that medical trainees who rely on AI before developing core clinical reasoning may never acquire those skills. The authors distinguish this from losing a skill one already had, or learning incorrect information from AI.

Never-skilling

Foundational skills never develop.

Deskilling

Previously acquired skills weaken.

Mis-skilling

Learners absorb incorrect information from AI.

The authors propose building competence without AI first, teaching students to evaluate AI critically, and then integrating it under supervision. They emphasize that direct evidence of never-skilling in medical training is still lacking and call for further research.

A related, separate perspective: In a New York Times opinion essay, geriatrician Rachael Bedard describes AI as useful in her own clinical practice while worrying that its speed may deprive trainees of the effortful reasoning through which clinical judgment develops. She discusses AI-free reasoning exercises and AI literacy as possible responses.

The essay and the Nature Medicine paper are separate works by different authors. Read together, they raise the question: how can medical education help students benefit from AI while still building the judgment needed to use it well?

2 / AI in the physical world

GPT Moves from Generating Content to Taking Action

GPT-6 Astra was connected to a car for a controlled DrivingBench test. It received camera observations and issued steering and speed commands through tools, completing a low-speed cone course on its second attempt. A human supervisor was ready to intervene, and the car's existing control and safety systems remained in use. This was a striking demonstration of GPT interacting with the physical world, but not a test of autonomous road driving.

My takeaway: This makes me think about how important Tesla's driving data is. A general-purpose model can now make a car move through a simple course, but robust driving across real-world situations depends on much more than a successful demonstration. Tesla's fleet data and task-specific training may matter most in building and improving that broader capability.

3 / Studying AI

Should We Study GPT Like a New Species?

GPT's internal workings can be difficult to interpret, while its behavior can be tested across many tasks and environments. Perhaps we can study it somewhat as biologists study unfamiliar organisms: design careful experiments, compare behavior under different conditions, and examine not just successes but also failure patterns. “New species” is a metaphor for how unfamiliar GPT can feel, not a literal biological claim.

1 / ProbeDesign a task

Test a specific ability.

2 / VaryChange conditions

Check whether behavior is robust.

3 / CompareRepeat and contrast

Compare models and versions.

4 / ExplainStudy failures

Look for shortcuts and limits.

My takeaway: We may need to study GPT as a new kind of system: observe it systematically, test it under varied conditions, and map where its abilities hold up or break down. We should not assume that behavior that looks human means the system thinks like a person or an animal.

4 / AI for biology

World Models for Biomedicine

This Cell review proposes that biomedical AI could move beyond predicting a measurement to modeling how a biological system changes over time and responds to interventions. The authors envision models spanning molecular, cellular, tissue, and clinical scales, combining observational data with longitudinal measurements and experimental perturbations.

What makes it a world model? It aims to predict how a system evolves after an action or intervention, so researchers can ask, “What might happen if we make this change, and what should we test next?”

Genomic prediction estimates genetic merit from genome-wide markers to rank selection candidates. A world model for breeding would go further: represent a population across generations, account for environment and management, and predict how different selection decisions might change future traits. Genomic prediction could be one part of that larger model.

AlphaFold 3 predicts molecular structures and interactions; by itself, it does not simulate how an entire biological system changes over time after an intervention. “Foundation model” describes a model trained on broad data that can be adapted to different tasks. It describes how a model is built, while “world model” describes the goal of predicting how a system evolves. These approaches can overlap, but they answer different questions.

My takeaway: A useful biomedical world model should help choose experiments by predicting what may happen after an intervention the model has not seen before. Those predictions still need to be tested against real experimental results.
