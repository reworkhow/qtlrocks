1 / Benchmarking AI

Can Deep Learning Predict Gene-Perturbation Effects?

Two recent papers appear to reach different conclusions about deep-learning models for predicting gene-perturbation effects. Their results are better read as complementary: one emphasizes the strength of simple baselines; the other shows that evaluation metrics can hide perturbation-specific signal.

Nature Methods · 2025

“Deep-learning-based gene perturbation effect prediction does not yet outperform simple linear baselines”

Testing unseen single and double perturbations, Ahlmann-Eltze et al. found that simple baselines were hard to beat. For double perturbations, the additive prediction from the two single perturbations outperformed the tested deep-learning models; for other tasks, simple mean or linear approaches were also highly competitive.

Read the paper

Nature Biotechnology · 2026

“Deep learning perturbation models can outperform baselines on calibrated metrics”

Miller et al. ask whether common metrics, such as whole-transcriptome MSE, are sensitive to the response genes that matter. They calibrate metrics using positive and negative controls and find that several models outperform uninformative baselines under weighted and rank-based metrics.

Read the paper

Why these findings can coexist: the first asks whether complex models beat strong simple baselines on the tested tasks; the second asks whether the metric can detect biologically informative predictions in the first place.

My takeaway: A larger model is not automatically a better biological model. But before declaring it a failure, check whether the benchmark uses strong baselines, appropriate data, and metrics that can detect the signal of interest.

Sources: Ahlmann-Eltze et al., Nature Methods Miller et al., Nature Biotechnology
2 / Integrating datasets

Connecting CattleGTEx with the Cattle Cell Atlas

CattleGTEx brings population-scale genetics; the Cattle Cell Atlas brings cellular resolution. Connecting them lets researchers ask which cell types may underlie a genetic signal found in a mixed tissue. The CattleCell-GTEx study combined atlas-based cell-composition estimates with genotype and bulk expression to identify cell-context-associated regulatory signals. This adds resolution, but does not by itself prove a causal mechanism.

From a cell atlas to bulk samples

Reference expression signatures help estimate cell-type contributions in each bulk-tissue sample:

yi ≈ S pi

S = reference signatures (genes × cell types); yi = bulk expression profile; pi = estimated cell composition for animal i. This schematic is not a literal cell count.

Then test whether the genotype-expression association varies with cell abundance:

Yi = μ + GiβG + PiβP + (GiPi)βGP + εi

The design matrix has one row per animal and columns for intercept, genotype, cell fraction, and genotype × cell fraction. The published analysis includes additional covariates and methods.

My takeaway: Integration is not simply stacking datasets. It uses one dataset's strengths to address a limitation of another, while keeping the inferential limits clear.

3 / AI for research infrastructure

scBaseCount: An AI-Curated, 502-Million-Cell Resource

Public single-cell data are abundant, but hard to find, interpret, and compare when metadata and processing vary across studies. This Cell paper introduces scBaseCount: an AI agent discovers 10x Genomics datasets in the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) and extracts metadata; a standardized pipeline then processes the data consistently.

Uniform processing supports comparable measurement of intronic and exonic reads and non-coding gene expression across studies. The authors present the resource as a broader foundation for meta-analysis and model training.

My takeaway: AI can contribute to science by building and maintaining research infrastructure, not only by answering questions after data are ready. Scale is valuable, but metadata quality, study representation, and experimental differences still deserve scrutiny.
