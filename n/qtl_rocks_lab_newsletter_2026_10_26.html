Find the Fun

Recently, we have talked a lot about AI agents, autonomy, and judgment. But maybe we should come back to a simpler answer: find the fun part.

When we enjoy investigating a question or building something, we want to start, try another approach, and see what happens. We are curious because exploring it is fun. AI can remove some tedious barriers and let us do more of that.

That does not mean every step must be enjoyable. A project can be difficult and frustrating while the exploration itself remains fun. The reason to pursue the question still has to be ours.

That is why I am adding a Fun Corner to the newsletter. It is a chance to explore something simply because it is fun, not because it will make us more productive or help us keep up with AI.

Fun Corner

Why Is It Called “Regression”?

We use regression all the time in R. But why a word that means going backward?

In “Regression Towards Mediocrity in Hereditary Stature” (1886), Francis Galton described a pattern: children of unusually tall parents tended to be tall, but less extreme on average. Children of unusually short parents showed the reverse. Here, “mediocrity” meant the population average, not poor quality. Original paper.

How to Read the Figure

The steeper “mid-parents” line represents the identity relationship. The shallower “children” line shows Galton’s fitted relationship: children’s average height deviation was approximately two-thirds of their mid-parents’ deviation.

For example, a mid-parent height 3 inches above average corresponded to an expected offspring height about 2 inches above average, using Galton’s sex-adjusted measurements. This describes an average, not every child.

The name stuck. Today, regression describes a much broader family of statistical models; it does not mean every relationship moves toward the mean.

Something Fun to Think About: If children regress toward the average, why doesn’t height variation disappear after many generations?
