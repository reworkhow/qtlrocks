Why Do We Divide Prediction Correlation by the Square Root of Heritability?

By Roddur | Student contribution | November 9, 2026

In genomic prediction, we often evaluate our models by looking at the correlation between genomic estimated breeding values (GEBVs) and observed phenotypes. But why do we sometimes divide that correlation by the square root of heritability? The answer comes down to the difference between predictive ability and prediction accuracy.

Predictive ability vs. prediction accuracy

Estimated genetic accuracy ≈ corr(GEBV, phenotype) / √h²

Predictive ability is the correlation between predicted breeding values (GEBVs) and observed phenotypes. Genetic prediction accuracy is the correlation between GEBVs and true breeding values.

The catch? We usually do not know the true breeding values. Instead, we validate against phenotypes, which include both additive genetic effects and residual variation. The square-root-of-heritability correction accounts for that difference under the usual assumptions.

Why √h², not h²?

Here's where the square root comes from. Start with a simple model for a phenotype:

y = g + e

Here, g is the true additive genetic value and e is the residual. Suppose they are uncorrelated. Then the covariance between the true breeding value and phenotype is:

Cov(g, y) = Cov(g, g + e)
= Var(g) + Cov(g, e)
= VA + 0 = VA

A correlation is covariance divided by the product of the standard deviations:

corr(g, y) = Cov(g, y) / (SD(g) × SD(y))
= VA / (√VA × √VP)
= √(VA / VP)
= √h²

So √h² is the correlation between the true breeding value and the phenotype in this simple model. It appears because correlations are scaled by standard deviations, not variances.

Now replace the unknown true breeding value with our prediction, GEBV (written as ĝ). If the GEBV is uncorrelated with the validation residual e, then:

Cov(ĝ, y) = Cov(ĝ, g + e)
= Cov(ĝ, g)
corr(ĝ, y) = Cov(ĝ, g) / (SD(ĝ) × SD(y))
= corr(ĝ, g) × SD(g) / SD(y)
= corr(ĝ, g) × √h²
corr(ĝ, g) = corr(ĝ, y) / √h²

That last line is the familiar heritability adjustment. We divide by √h² because we are converting a correlation with a noisy phenotype into an estimate of the correlation with its additive genetic value.

A simple example

Suppose we have a trait with heritability 0.25, and our GEBVs have a correlation of 0.35 with the observed phenotypes.

Estimated accuracy = 0.35 / √0.25
= 0.35 / 0.50
= 0.70

So even though our model correlates only 0.35 with the phenotypes, its estimated correlation with the true breeding values is 0.70.

Why does this make sense? We are trying to predict the genetic part of the trait, not the residual variation included in the phenotype.

Take-home message

Predictive ability tells us how well we predict observed phenotypes; genetic prediction accuracy tells us how well we predict true breeding values. Dividing by √h² helps connect them because phenotypes are imperfect indicators of true breeding values.

One important caution: This adjustment assumes an appropriate heritability estimate, that GEBVs are uncorrelated with validation residuals, and a suitable validation design without information leakage. It is an estimate, not a guaranteed accuracy, and should not be used when true breeding values are already known (for example, in a simulation).
